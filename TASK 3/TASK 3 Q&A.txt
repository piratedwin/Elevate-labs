1. What is Vulnerability Scanning?
Vulnerability scanning is the automated process of identifying security weaknesses in systems, networks, or applications. Tools probe for misconfigurations, outdated software, or exploitable flaws.

2. Difference Between Vulnerability Scanning and Penetration Testing
Vulnerability Scanning: Automated, broad, and identifies potential issues. It’s like a health check-up.
Penetration Testing: Manual or semi-automated, simulates real-world attacks to exploit vulnerabilities. It’s like a stress test.
Scanning finds what could be wrong, pen testing shows what can actually be broken.

3. Common Vulnerabilities in Personal Computers
Outdated operating systems or software.
Weak or reused passwords.
Missing patches for browsers, plugins, or drivers.
Misconfigured firewalls or antivirus.
Unencrypted sensitive files.

4. How Do Scanners Detect Vulnerabilities?
Signature-based checks: Compare system details against known vulnerability databases.
Banner grabbing: Identify software versions from service responses.
Configuration analysis: Detect weak settings (e.g., default passwords).
Patch verification: Check if critical updates are missing.

5. What is CVSS?
The Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) is a standardized framework for rating the severity of vulnerabilities.
Scores range from 0.0 (none) to 10.0 (critical).
Factors include exploitability, impact, and environmental context.

6. How Often Should Vulnerability Scans Be Performed?
Best practice: At least monthly or quarterly.
Critical systems: Weekly or continuous scanning.
After changes: Run scans after major updates, new deployments, or incidents.

7. What is a False Positive in Vulnerability Scanning?
A false positive occurs when a scanner flags a vulnerability that doesn’t actually exist.
Example: Reporting a service as outdated when it’s patched but misreports its version.
Manual validation helps reduce noise.

8. How Do You Prioritize Vulnerabilities?
Severity: Use CVSS scores (critical first).
Exploit availability: Focus on flaws with known exploits.
Asset value: Prioritize systems holding sensitive data.
Business impact: Consider downtime, compliance, or customer trust.
Compensating controls: If mitigations exist (e.g., firewall rules), urgency may be lower.
