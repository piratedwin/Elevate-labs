1. What is Phishing?
Phishing is a fraudulent attempt to obtain sensitive information (like passwords, credit card numbers, or personal data) by disguising as a trustworthy entity, usually through email, SMS, or fake websites.

2. How to Identify a Phishing Email
Suspicious sender address (slight misspellings, odd domains).
Urgent or threatening language (“Your account will be locked!”).
Unexpected attachments or links.
Poor grammar or spelling mistakes.
Generic greetings (“Dear Customer” instead of your name).

3. What is Email Spoofing?
Email spoofing is when attackers forge the sender’s address to make an email look like it comes from a trusted source. It’s often used in phishing to trick recipients into believing the message is legitimate.

4. Why Are Phishing Emails Dangerous?
They can steal login credentials.
Spread malware via attachments or links.
Lead to financial fraud or identity theft.
Compromise entire organizations if one employee clicks.

5. How Can You Verify the Sender’s Authenticity?
Check the full email header for the real sending domain.
Hover over links to see the actual URL.
Contact the sender through official channels (not by replying).
Use DKIM, SPF, and DMARC records to validate email authenticity.

6. What Tools Can Analyze Email Headers?
MxToolbox (online header analyzer).
Google Admin Toolbox.
Microsoft Message Header Analyzer (Outlook add-in).
Command-line tools like exim or sendmail logs for deeper inspection. we can also use splunk tool to check for the logs

7. What Actions Should Be Taken on Suspected Phishing Emails?
Do not click links or open attachments.
Report to your IT/security team.
Block the sender/domain.
Delete the email after reporting.
If clicked, immediately change passwords and run malware scans.

8. How Do Attackers Use Social Engineering in Phishing?
Impersonation: Pretending to be a boss, bank, or government agency.
Urgency: Pressuring victims to act quickly without thinking.
Fear or reward: Threatening account closure or promising prizes.
Trust exploitation: Using familiar logos, names, or language to lower suspicion.
